## Spoken narration draft

At first, the river seems empty. The surface barely moves. Then, just above the waterline, two eyes appear.

That small detail changes the whole picture. The crocodile is not simply floating with its head exposed. It can position much of its body below the surface while keeping its eyes, ears and nostrils near the air-water boundary. From the bank, that can make a very large animal surprisingly difficult to see.

But the eyes are only the part we notice. The real story is a combination of body design, patience, and a sensory system built for life between water and land.

The Nile crocodile, Crocodylus niloticus, is one of Africa's major aquatic predators. It lives in a range of freshwater environments, including rivers, lakes and swamps. It is not restricted to one kind of river scene, and its behaviour changes with its age, size, habitat and the food available.

A young crocodile and a large adult do not have identical diets or hunting opportunities. Smaller animals take smaller prey, including insects and aquatic invertebrates. As they grow, their opportunities expand. Larger Nile crocodiles can take fish, reptiles, birds and mammals when those animals are within reach. They are opportunistic predators, not specialists that depend on one dramatic hunting trick.

That matters because the famous waterline posture is not a magic disguise. It is one useful way of remaining concealed while monitoring what is happening around the water.

Look at the arrangement. The eyes sit high on the head. The nostrils are positioned so the animal can breathe while most of its body remains submerged. The ears are also close to the top of the head. When a crocodile holds this position, it can keep important senses available without exposing its entire body.

The posture also changes what an observer sees. From a low angle, the water hides the broad back, the legs and much of the tail. Reflections, ripples, vegetation and muddy water can make the outline even harder to read. A viewer may register two eyes and assume that is almost the whole animal. Under the surface, it is not.

There is another part of the story that is easy to underestimate: crocodiles do not rely on sight alone to understand their watery surroundings.

Crocodilians have specialised sensory organs in their skin, commonly called integumentary sensory organs. These mechanoreceptors respond to mechanical changes, including disturbances at the water surface and movement through the water. Research on Nile crocodiles has tested their responses to surface waves and found that they can detect very small wave signals under experimental conditions.

That does not mean a crocodile can identify every animal from an impossible distance, or that every tiny ripple automatically triggers an attack. The research describes a sensory capability, not a supernatural radar system. The important point is more grounded: the water itself carries information, and the crocodile has specialised equipment for detecting some of it.

A disturbance can tell an animal that something has moved nearby. But detection is not the same as identification, and neither one guarantees a strike. A crocodile still has to orient, assess, and decide whether to move. A recent study of Nile crocodiles examined how they integrate different signals arriving close together in time, including sound and water-surface vibration. It is a reminder that hunting is not just a reflex to one ripple. It involves processing information from a changing environment.

So how does the ambush fit together?

In a typical shoreline ambush, the crocodile may remain in the water near a place where animals approach the bank. It can keep a low profile, watch from the surface, and wait for an opportunity. When prey comes within reach, a rapid launch can carry the head and front of the body out of the water. The strike is brief compared with the waiting that comes before it.

The crocodile's powerful tail provides its main swimming thrust. Its streamlined body helps it move through water, while the limbs can be held close to the body during swimming. These features help it approach or reposition without the kind of conspicuous movement a land animal might make.

The attack itself should not be confused with a long chase. Crocodiles often depend on opportunity and surprise. A large animal at the edge of the water may be vulnerable for only a moment: while drinking, crossing a narrow channel, or moving through a place where the bank meets deep water. The exact behaviour varies, and not every crocodile uses the same hunting position every time.

After a successful capture, the story changes again. Crocodiles are adapted to handling prey in water. Large prey may be dragged or held underwater, and feeding can involve powerful movements that are very different from the quick strike seen at the surface. It is not necessary to show graphic footage to understand the mechanics. A restrained sequence of underwater movement and a clear explanation are enough.

There is also a reason not to turn every crocodile scene into a monster story. These animals are part of functioning freshwater ecosystems. They feed on a range of prey, interact with other species, and occupy habitats that change with water levels and seasons. Their presence is a natural part of those systems, even though an encounter with a large crocodile can be extremely dangerous for people.

That danger is not theoretical. The IUCN Crocodile Specialist Group warns that crocodilians can remain submerged for long periods, move between water bodies, and hunt close to shore. Not seeing a crocodile does not prove that a stretch of water is safe. A quiet surface is not a safety signal.

The practical rule is simple: never approach a crocodile shoreline, never enter water known to contain crocodiles, and follow local wildlife authority guidance and warning signs. Do not try to get closer for a photograph or test how near an animal will allow you to stand. Keep a safe distance and let trained wildlife professionals manage any necessary observation.

Return to those two eyes above the surface. They are not the whole animal, and they are not proof that an attack is about to happen. They are a visible clue to a much more complete system: a body adapted to water, senses tuned to movement, and a hunting strategy that can depend on remaining unseen until the moment matters.

The river may look empty. That does not mean it is empty. And understanding the crocodile is a better response than underestimating it.

**End card:** “Respect the water. Keep your distance.”

## Research and claim map

1. **Species, habitats and broad ecology** — Nile crocodiles use rivers, lakes and freshwater swamps; diet shifts with age and includes a broad range of prey.  
   Source: IUCN/SSC Crocodile Specialist Group, *Crocodylus niloticus* Action Plan (2010): https://iucncsg.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Crocodylus-niloticus-Action-Plan-2010.pdf

2. **Minimum-exposure posture and sensory organs** — crocodilians can hold the head at the air-water interface with eyes, nostrils and ears above the surface; integumentary sensory organs detect water-surface disturbance and underwater displacement.  
   Source: *Temporal integration and decision-making in crocodiles*, Biology Open (2025): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12079573/

3. **Nile crocodile surface-wave sensitivity** — experimental work tested responses to capillary surface waves; describe this as a demonstrated sensory capability, not unlimited detection range.  
   Source: Grap et al., *Crocodylus niloticus is highly sensitive to water surface waves*, Zoology (2015): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26153334/

4. **Shoreline ambush behaviour** — crocodiles may approach or wait near the shore, keep eyes above water to check position, and launch toward prey; avoid implying every hunt follows this exact sequence.  
   Source: San Diego Zoo Wildlife Explorers Center, Nile Crocodile — Diet & Feeding: https://ielc.libguides.com/sdzg/factsheets/nile_crocodile/diet

5. **Human safety** — crocodilians can remain submerged, move between water bodies and hunt close to shore; absence of a visible animal is not proof of safety.  
   Source: IUCN/SSC Crocodile Specialist Group, Crocodilian Attacks: https://www.iucncsg.org/pages/Crocodilian-Attacks.html
