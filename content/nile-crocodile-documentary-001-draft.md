## Narration and scene plan

### Opening — The hidden predator (0:00–0:45)

A river can appear completely still.

But beneath its surface, one of Africa's most powerful predators may be watching.

Only a pair of eyes, nostrils and the upper part of a head may break the water.

The rest of the animal can remain hidden.

This is the Nile crocodile. An animal whose survival depends on a remarkable combination of patience, physical adaptation and an intimate relationship with its aquatic environment.

Today, we look beyond the fear surrounding this animal to understand how it lives, hunts, reproduces and survives.

**Visuals:** Real river establishing shot; authentic crocodile head at water level; no staged or generated encounter.

### Chapter 1 — Built for the water (0:45–1:50)

The Nile crocodile is one of Africa's largest crocodilians.

Its body is protected by tough skin and reinforced bony structures. Its long, powerful tail supplies most of the forward thrust when swimming.

Look closely at the head.

The eyes, ears and nostrils are positioned high on the skull. This arrangement allows the crocodile to remain mostly submerged while keeping important sensory openings above the water.

Its nostrils can close underwater, helping prevent water from entering the respiratory passages.

These adaptations allow the animal to move between the surface and the underwater environment without needing to expose its entire body.

It is not simply a large animal floating in a river. Its anatomy is specialised for an aquatic way of life.

**Visuals:** Genuine head close-up; underwater or side-on swimming footage only where creator rights and provenance are confirmed.

### Chapter 2 — A changing diet (1:50–2:55)

A Nile crocodile does not eat the same things throughout its life.

Very young crocodiles feed largely on insects and other small invertebrates.

As they grow, their opportunities change. Small fish, amphibians, crustaceans and other vertebrates become part of the diet.

Larger crocodiles can take much bigger prey, including fish and terrestrial mammals.

Fish are an important part of the diet in many habitats.

The crocodile is also capable of scavenging when suitable carcasses are available.

This change in feeding behaviour is an example of how growth transforms an animal's place in its ecosystem.

**Visuals:** Juvenile and adult footage must be correctly identified; do not use a different crocodilian species as a stand-in.

### Chapter 3 — The ambush strategy (2:55–4:05)

The Nile crocodile is an ambush predator.

Rather than spending energy continuously pursuing prey, it can remain still for extended periods.

When prey comes within reach, a crocodile may launch a sudden attack.

Its jaws are powerful, but the entire body contributes to controlling and handling large prey.

The tail helps drive the animal through the water, while the body and limbs assist with movement and positioning.

Not every crocodile seen near a riverbank is hunting. Some are resting, regulating their body temperature or simply moving between suitable areas.

The difference matters.

We should describe what the animal is actually doing, rather than assigning a motive that the footage cannot establish.

**Visuals:** Use only actual recorded behaviour; do not edit unrelated shots into a false sequence.

### Chapter 4 — Life begins on land (4:05–5:15)

Although the Nile crocodile spends much of its life in and around water, reproduction requires a different environment.

Females dig nests in suitable sandy ground, generally on riverbanks or other appropriate sites.

They lay eggs and guard the nesting area.

Temperature during incubation influences the sex of developing crocodiles.

When the eggs hatch, the young are particularly vulnerable.

Predators, environmental conditions and access to suitable habitat all influence their chances of survival.

Parental care is an important part of this story. Female Nile crocodiles can assist hatchlings in reaching the water and protect their young against potential threats.

For an animal so often portrayed only as a solitary predator, this is a remarkable part of its natural history.

**Visuals:** Nesting, hatchling and maternal-care scenes require original, correctly identified footage; if unavailable, omit the scene rather than simulate it.

### Chapter 5 — Living alongside a powerful predator (5:15–6:30)

Nile crocodiles occupy rivers, lakes, wetlands and other suitable aquatic habitats across much of sub-Saharan Africa.

They are important predators, but their presence can also create serious challenges for people.

Where communities depend on rivers for fishing, farming, livestock and daily activities, encounters can be dangerous.

A crocodile does not need to be visibly aggressive to represent a risk.

People should never enter crocodile-inhabited water unnecessarily, approach riverbanks carelessly or attempt to feed or handle wild crocodiles.

Safe distances and local wildlife guidance matter.

Conservation must recognise both the ecological importance of crocodiles and the real safety concerns of the people who share their habitats.

**Visuals:** Genuine habitat and community context only; avoid sensationalised or unverified attack imagery.

### Chapter 6 — Conservation and the future (6:30–7:25)

The global IUCN assessment linked by the Crocodile Specialist Group (2017 assessment) lists the Nile crocodile as Least Concern. That does not mean every population is secure.

South Africa's 2023 regional assessment lists the species as Vulnerable.

Habitat changes, human conflict and local population declines remain important concerns.

Understanding the difference between global and regional conservation status is essential.

Protecting crocodiles requires reliable population monitoring, suitable wetland habitats, responsible management and better awareness of how people can live more safely alongside them.

**Visuals:** Real wetlands, habitat and conservation work; verify location and date before adding location-specific claims.

### Closing — Respect what the river conceals (7:25–7:55)

The Nile crocodile is more than a frightening silhouette beneath the water.

It is a highly adapted reptile with a complex life cycle, specialised hunting abilities and an important ecological role.

The next time you look across an African river, remember that what you see on the surface may tell only a small part of the story.

Observe wildlife with respect. Keep your distance. And never mistake a quiet river for a safe one.
