## Narration script and visual plan

### 0:00–0:25 — Hook

**Voice-over:**  
You can have stunning wildlife footage and still end up with a video that feels forgettable. The missing piece is often not a better camera or a more dramatic animal. It is a story. In this video, we’ll turn a folder of clips into a clear mini-documentary using a simple three-part structure: set up a question, show what the footage actually reveals, and finish with a meaningful payoff.

**Visuals:**  
Open on three distinct, rights-cleared shots: a wide habitat view, an animal behaving naturally, and a detail shot. Cut to a blank three-part timeline. Avoid fabricated animal encounters.

**On-screen text:** “SETUP → DEVELOPMENT → PAYOFF”

### 0:25–1:15 — Step 1: Find the question

**Voice-over:**  
Before you edit, watch every clip and write down what is genuinely visible. Is the animal searching, resting, moving through its habitat, feeding, grooming, or responding to something in the environment? Do not begin with a sensational headline and force the footage to fit it. Start with a question the material can answer. For example: “How does this animal use its surroundings?” or “What changes over the course of this sequence?” If the clips do not show the answer, choose a narrower question.

**Visuals:**  
Show a simple clip log with columns for filename, visible action, location if known, usable duration, audio, and source/licence. Use placeholder footage only in the edit mock-up until licensed media is selected.

**On-screen text:** “Let the footage set the limits.”

### 1:15–2:10 — Step 2: Build a beginning, middle and end

**Voice-over:**  
Your beginning gives viewers orientation: where are we, what animal are we observing, and what should we pay attention to? The middle develops the question with the strongest relevant shots. The ending gives viewers a satisfying final observation or returns to the original question. This does not mean every animal sequence needs a dramatic conflict. A quiet observation can have a real payoff when the edit helps us notice something we might otherwise miss.

Try arranging clips in three bins: establish, develop, resolve. If a shot does not help the question, the context, or the rhythm, leave it out.

**Visuals:**  
Demonstrate three labelled bins on a timeline. Use a habitat wide shot for establish, several relevant details for develop, and a natural concluding shot for resolve.

### 2:10–3:05 — Step 3: Edit for meaning, not just speed

**Voice-over:**  
A fast cut is not automatically an interesting cut. Give viewers enough time to understand what they are seeing. Use a wide shot to establish space, a medium shot to show the animal’s action, and close details when they add useful information. Keep the real order of events when sequence matters. Do not rearrange separate moments to make it look as though one animal is reacting to another when that was not recorded.

Remove repeated shots, dead time that adds nothing, and transitions that distract from the subject. Then watch the edit without narration. If the sequence is confusing with the sound off, the visual order probably needs work.

**Visuals:**  
Show a timeline before and after trimming. Use neutral labels such as “wide”, “action”, and “detail”. Do not manufacture a reaction shot.

### 3:05–4:00 — Step 4: Let natural sound do some work

**Voice-over:**  
If the recording contains useful natural sound, protect it. Wind, footsteps, water, insects, or birds can make a scene feel present, but only use sounds that genuinely belong to the clip or are clearly identified as added sound design. Reduce distracting hiss or wind carefully; aggressive noise removal can make audio sound unnatural. Music is optional. If you add it, keep it below speech and make sure the licence covers your intended use.

Leave a little breathing room between important lines. Constant music, effects and narration can tire viewers and hide details that make wildlife footage special.

**Visuals:**  
Show audio tracks labelled “camera audio”, “voice-over”, and “licensed music”. Demonstrate ducking music under speech with simple level automation.

### 4:00–5:00 — Step 5: Narrate only what you can support

**Voice-over:**  
Narration should add context, not describe every visible movement. If the picture already shows an animal walking, you do not need to say, “The animal is walking.” Instead, explain why the shot matters, provide verified context, or ask a useful question.

Be careful with words such as “hunts”, “escapes”, “protects”, or “attacks”. Those words imply intent or an event. Use them only when the footage and trustworthy research support the claim. If you do not know the location, age, sex, or motivation of the animal, do not guess. Keep a source note for every factual claim and remove anything you cannot verify.

**Visuals:**  
On screen, compare “The animal is terrified” with “The animal pauses and looks toward the edge of the frame.” Mark the second as observable description; do not assert a cause without evidence.

### 5:00–5:55 — Step 6: Make the title and thumbnail honest

**Voice-over:**  
Packaging should create curiosity about the story that is actually in the video. Choose one clear subject, one readable visual idea and only a few words if text helps. A thumbnail can be dramatic without inventing an event. The title and opening should match: if you promise a feeding sequence, viewers should see that sequence, not a long unrelated introduction.

Before export, ask someone who has not seen the edit what they think the video is about after seeing the thumbnail and first twenty seconds. If their expectation does not match your video, fix the packaging or the edit.

**Visuals:**  
Show a simple thumbnail mock-up beside the opening shot. Add a checklist: “Accurate? Readable? Matches the video?”

### 5:55–6:50 — Step 7: Final quality and rights check

**Voice-over:**  
Before you publish, check the full video on a phone and a larger screen. Listen with headphones for clipped narration, abrupt music changes and distracting background noise. Check captions against the final voice track, not an earlier draft. Verify the spelling of species names and every factual statement. Keep a record of where each clip, image, sound effect and music track came from and what its licence permits.

If you use AI-generated or altered material, make sure it does not mislead viewers about real animal behaviour. Review YouTube’s current disclosure requirements and select the appropriate settings. If you promote a product or use a commercial relationship, disclose it clearly and use the platform’s paid-promotion setting when required.

**Visuals:**  
Show a pre-publication checklist and a folder containing licence records, source notes, final captions and export.

### 6:50–7:25 — Recap and call to action

**Voice-over:**  
Here is the method: choose a question the footage can answer, arrange the best shots into a beginning, middle and end, edit for meaning, preserve useful natural sound, verify the narration, and make the packaging match the actual story. You do not need to make every video louder or more dramatic. You need to make each shot earn its place.

If this helped, subscribe to SonicVhibez for practical creator workflows. And if you want a planning resource for wildlife video creation, the Wildlife Creator’s Vault link is in the description. Check the product details before purchasing. Thanks for watching.

**Visuals:**  
Return to the three-part timeline, then a restrained end screen with subscribe prompt and one relevant video placeholder. Only display the product link if its details and promotional disclosures have been verified.

## Editorial and compliance checklist

- [ ] Confirm all footage is licensed for the intended YouTube/commercial use; save licence evidence and source URLs.
- [ ] Confirm that clips are not edited to imply an event or reaction that did not happen.
- [ ] Research and cite any factual animal-behaviour claims in the production notes; remove unsupported claims.
- [ ] Confirm the product link opens the intended product and verify whether a paid/affiliate relationship applies.
- [ ] Confirm the video is original, adds educational value, and is not repetitive or mass-produced.
- [ ] Generate final narration; target 7–8 minutes, but do not stretch with filler to hit duration.
- [ ] Create captions from the final narration and check timing.
- [ ] Render and inspect the actual 16:9 MP4; verify audio, picture, title, thumbnail and end screen.
- [ ] Apply relevant YouTube altered/synthetic-content and paid-promotion settings.
- [ ] Human review and approval required before upload or scheduling.
