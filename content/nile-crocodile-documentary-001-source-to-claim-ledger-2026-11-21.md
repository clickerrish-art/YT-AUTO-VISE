## Scope and limitations

This desk review checks whether the draft's main natural-history statements are supported by authoritative species references and published research. ## Claim-by-claim review

| Script section / claim | Evidence located | Assessment and required editorial treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Species identity and size: Nile crocodile is a very large African crocodilian | IUCN Crocodile Specialist Group (CSG) species account describes *Crocodylus niloticus* as one of the largest crocodilians; females may reach 3 m, males average over 4 m and occasionally exceed 5 m. | Supported in broad terms. Prefer “one of Africa's largest crocodilians” over “one of Africa's largest living reptiles”; do not present maximum sizes as typical. |
| Armour and aquatic adaptations | CSG describes heavy armour. SDZWA physical-characteristics fact sheet places eyes, ears and nostrils on top of the head and says the nostrils pinch shut underwater. SDZWA behaviour fact sheet describes only eyes, ears and nostrils exposed at the surface. | Supported. Use “bony armour/scutes” only if the reviewer confirms the anatomical terminology. Do not imply the animal is invisible or safe because most of its body is submerged. |
| Tail provides swimming thrust | SDZWA Library / San Diego Zoo Wildlife Alliance behaviour and ecology fact sheet describes the strong tail as the main forward thrust for swimming. | Supported. Use genuine swimming footage if available; do not use unrelated underwater shots as proof of a particular movement. |
| Diet changes with age | IUCN CSG says juveniles feed on insects and small fish and describes a broad diet. SDZWA Diet & Feeding says prey changes as crocodiles grow; youngest juveniles are insectivorous, older juveniles take more small vertebrates/invertebrates, fish are a mainstay for adults, and scavenging occurs. | Supported in broad terms. Avoid implying every individual follows an identical diet or that any particular prey item is common everywhere. |
| Ambush strategy and prey capture | SDZWA Diet & Feeding describes shoreline ambush, underwater approach, surfacing with eyes above water to check position, lunging, dragging prey into water and dismemberment. | Supported. Keep descriptions conditional (“may”). Never edit unrelated clips into a false attack or feeding sequence. |
| Resting and basking near riverbanks | SDZWA Behaviour & Ecology describes daytime basking, cooling in shade and long periods submerged. | Supported. The script's warning that a riverbank crocodile is not necessarily hunting is appropriate; distinguish observable behaviour from inferred intent. |
| Nesting on sandy ground near water | IUCN CSG describes females using sandy soil on elevated sites along broad river banks. SDZWA Reproduction & Development describes sandy sites near permanent water, including river flats and lake-side beaches. | Supported. Do not identify a nest in footage unless species, location and behaviour are verified by the creator or reviewer. |
| Egg clutch size and incubation duration | IUCN CSG species account gives 35–50 eggs and 75–95 days. SDZWA Reproduction & Development gives 55–60 eggs and 84–90 days. | Reference figures differ. The current script wisely avoids exact figures. Keep it that way unless a qualified reviewer resolves the population/source context. |
| Incubation temperature influences hatchling sex | SDZWA Reproduction & Development says nest temperature influences sex and reports temperature ranges from cited studies. Leslie's Nile crocodile research describes temperature-dependent sex determination, including a female–male–female pattern for the Lake St Lucia ecosystem. | Supported, but do not quote simple universal thresholds. Temperature response can be population/context dependent; qualified review required before adding numbers. |
| Female nest guarding and hatchling assistance | SDZWA Reproduction & Development describes nest guarding and hatchling assistance. Combrink, Warner & Downs (2016) studied 19 Lake St Lucia nests over four years; five females were observed freeing hatchlings, and a detailed sequence recorded 13 trips between nest and water over 32.5 hours. | Strongly supported for observed South African cases. Say females “can” guard nests and assist hatchlings; do not imply identical care in every nest or population. |
| Distribution and habitat | IUCN CSG species account describes rivers, lakes, wetlands and reservoirs across sub-Saharan Africa, including southern/eastern Africa and Madagascar. | Supported. Avoid saying “every African river” or suggesting presence at a specific filming location without location evidence. |
| Human-crocodile conflict and safety | IUCN CSG lists human-crocodile conflict, attacks on people/livestock, habitat alteration and pollution among relevant issues. | Supported in broad terms. Avoid sensational statistics or unsupported attack claims. Obtain current South African park/conservation authority safety wording before adding local instructions or distances. |
| Global and South African conservation status | IUCN CSG species account links the global Least Concern assessment (2017 assessment). SANBI's *Suricata 10* (2023 assessment) lists the South African regional population as Vulnerable A2ac. | Supported, with dates essential. State “global assessment: Least Concern (2017)” and “South African regional assessment: Vulnerable (2023)”. Do not present the regional category as the global category or imply a 2026 reassessment occurred. |

## Specific script edits recommended before zoological review

1. Change “one of Africa's largest living reptiles” to “one of Africa's largest crocodilians” for taxonomic precision.
2. Keep the clutch-size and incubation-day numbers out of the narration until a qualified reviewer resolves the differing source figures.
3. Keep the temperature-dependent sex statement general; do not add exact Celsius thresholds without specialist approval.
4. Keep “females can assist hatchlings” conditional and, if useful, identify the Lake St Lucia study as a documented South African example.
5. Attach the assessment years to global and South African regional conservation categories.
6. Keep prey capture and attack language conditional. Do not use a clip to imply hunting, attack, feeding or a specific prey event unless the original recording establishes it.
7. Add the SDZWA reproduction fact sheet and the Combrink, Warner & Downs 2016 nest/maternal-care study to the script's source references.
8. Obtain a qualified zoological reviewer’s written sign-off against the final script revision and source-to-claim ledger.
